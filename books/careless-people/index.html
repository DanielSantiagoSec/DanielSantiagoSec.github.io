Why I read it

This one came up in conversations about the intersection of tech, governance, and power — territory I care about as someone building toward AI security and policy work. A former Meta policy director writing about what it actually looked like from the inside? That's primary source material. Found it free on Hoopla through my library — worth checking before you buy.

My take

Sarah Wynn-Williams didn't walk into Facebook as a tech person. She came from diplomacy — she'd worked as a New Zealand diplomat, including in Washington, D.C. She saw Facebook as a genuine tool for democratic reach: the ability to connect communities, mobilize people, cross borders that traditional media couldn't. She believed in the potential. That belief is what makes the book hit the way it does, because you spend the whole thing watching it get dismantled piece by piece.

The core argument of the book isn't that Facebook was evil from the start. It's that the people at the top accumulated power faster than they developed any framework for what to do with it responsibly. Wynn-Williams describes what she calls a "lethal carelessness" — not malice in every case, but a consistent pattern of making consequential decisions about billions of people's lives with the same energy you'd use to push a product update. Countries destabilized. Elections influenced. Content moderation policies that applied differently depending on whether the government involved was useful to Facebook's expansion goals. All of it handled with a shrug toward the downstream effects.

The workplace layer runs parallel to all of this. Wynn-Williams was one of very few women in senior positions, navigating pregnancy, returning from maternity leave, and fielding the kind of treatment that doesn't get documented in HR reports — the subtle and not-so-subtle signals about what kind of person is taken seriously in that room. She reported harassment. She was ultimately fired. The personal and political threads don't feel like separate stories — they're the same story about what happens when accountability is optional at every level.

What connects this to my own work is the governance question. As someone building toward AI security and policy, the pattern Wynn-Williams describes is exactly what the field is trying to prevent from repeating: powerful systems deployed at global scale, governed by people who are optimizing for growth without asking who carries the cost. The book ends with her working on AI policy — not as a pivot, but as a direct continuation of everything she learned watching Facebook operate without guardrails. That thread matters.

Practitioner takeaways

⚖️

Power without accountability is the actual threat model. Not just external actors. The most consequential damage in this book came from decisions made internally, by people with no external check on them.

🌍

Policy decisions at scale have real-world casualties. Wynn-Williams documents countries where Facebook's content and moderation choices contributed directly to real-world harm. That's not hypothetical risk. That's what "move fast" looks like at a billion-user scale.

📋

Governance frameworks matter before the crisis, not after. The absence of real policy infrastructure — not rules on paper, but actual enforced accountability — is what allowed the pattern to repeat across countries and contexts.

🤖

The AI governance problem is the same problem, next chapter. Wynn-Williams didn't leave tech — she moved into AI policy work. The lesson she took from Facebook was that if governance doesn't exist at the start, it won't exist when it's needed most.

🎤

Speaking up has a cost — and she paid it. She documented. She reported. She was fired. That's an honest portrait of what institutional accountability actually looks like when the institution isn't interested in accountability.

Bottom line

This book is relevant to anyone building in tech who wants to understand what "responsible development" actually has to mean in practice — not as a values statement but as a structural requirement. Wynn-Williams lived the cost of what happens when that requirement is missing. If you're going to work in AI governance, security policy, or anything adjacent to how powerful systems get deployed at scale, this is required reading.
