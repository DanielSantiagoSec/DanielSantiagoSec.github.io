# What Facebook Taught One Woman About Power — And What It Should Teach the Rest of Us **Careless People by Sarah Wynn-Williams | Practitioner Review** --- Meta tried to legally block this book from being promoted. The author was ordered by an arbitrator to stop speaking about it publicly. The publisher refused to pull it. The internet found out. Careless People hit #1 on the New York Times bestseller list, and Wynn-Williams was eventually invited to testify before the U.S. Senate. That sequence of events is its own argument for why the book matters. When one of the most powerful companies on the planet tries to silence a former employee's account of what she witnessed, and the response is to make it a bestseller, something is being said about accountability — or the absence of it — that goes beyond any individual chapter of the book. I read this while I was in the middle of a heavy interview process, picking it up on Hoopla and honestly using it as an anchor to why the work I'm building toward matters. It's the kind of book that earns its weight. --- ## She wasn't a tech person. That's the point. Sarah Wynn-Williams came to Facebook from diplomacy. She had worked as a New Zealand diplomat, including postings in Washington, D.C. She understood how governments worked, how policy got made, how information moved across borders. She saw Facebook as a genuine instrument for democratic participation — a way to reach communities that traditional media couldn't, to connect movements, to give voice to people who didn't have institutional megaphones. She believed in the potential. That belief is load-bearing in this book, because everything that follows is the story of watching it get used as justification for decisions that had nothing to do with democratic participation and everything to do with market access. The title comes from F. Scott Fitzgerald's The Great Gatsby — Tom and Daisy Buchanan, who "smashed up things and creatures and then retreated back into their money or their vast carelessness." Wynn-Williams is precise about this choice. The problem at Facebook, as she documents it, was not always deliberate malice. It was carelessness at scale. People making consequential decisions about billions of lives with the same urgency and the same accountability framework they would apply to a product sprint. --- ## What "lethal carelessness" actually looks like The phrase she uses for it is "lethal carelessness" — and she means it literally in some cases. The book documents countries where Facebook's content moderation choices and expansion decisions contributed to real-world political violence. Not as a side effect that nobody anticipated, but as an outcome that was reported internally, flagged, and deprioritized because the market was valuable. She was in the room for some of it. She describes encounters with juntas, with governments whose cooperation Facebook needed to maintain market access, with leaders whose willingness to use Facebook's tools for their own political consolidation was understood and accommodated. None of this was secret inside the company. The question that never got adequately answered was: what do we owe the people on the receiving end of this? The answer, as she documents it, was usually: less than whatever the next growth metric required. This is the accountability problem in its most concrete form. Not a question of values statements or mission language — Facebook had plenty of both — but of what actually happened when the values ran into the growth targets. Wynn-Williams watched the pattern repeat enough times to know it wasn't a series of mistakes. It was a structure. --- ## The workplace thread is the same story Running parallel to all of this is her experience as one of very few women in senior positions at the company. She navigated pregnancy and returning from maternity leave inside an environment that made clear, in ways that rarely got documented, which bodies and life circumstances fit the model of a serious person. She reported her boss, Joel Kaplan, for sexual harassment. She was eventually fired. She believes those facts are connected, and she makes the case for why with the same methodical attention she brings to the policy chapters. I want to be direct about why this thread matters to how I read the book: it's the same story. The carelessness that allowed a company to shrug at the political consequences of its decisions in Myanmar is the same carelessness that allowed workplace dynamics to operate without meaningful accountability. Both reflect an environment where the people at the top had accumulated power faster than they developed any obligation to use it responsibly — toward the countries Facebook operated in, and toward the people who worked there. That's not a coincidence. It's a culture. --- ## The governance question Wynn-Williams didn't leave tech after Facebook. She moved into AI policy work — and she frames it explicitly as a continuation, not a pivot. The lesson she took from watching Facebook operate without structural guardrails is that the time to build accountability frameworks is before the platform has a billion users, not after the Senate starts asking questions. This is where the book lands for me as someone building toward AI security and governance. The pattern she describes is exactly what the field is trying to prevent from repeating: powerful systems deployed at global scale, governed by people who are optimizing for one variable with no structural requirement to account for the costs they're externalizing onto everyone else. She raises the question of human oversight in high-stakes decisions — nuclear situations are mentioned, but the principle extends everywhere that consequential automated decisions get made at speed and scale. Who is accountable? To whom? Under what mechanism? These aren't rhetorical questions in 2025. They're the questions AI governance is supposed to be answering, and we are not moving as fast as the systems we're trying to govern. --- ## What I took from it Careless People is not a comfortable book. It's not written to make you feel good about where things ended up, or confident that the right people are learning the right lessons. Wynn-Williams is a precise and often darkly funny writer, which makes the harder sections land harder. What it gave me was a clearer picture of what accountability actually has to mean in practice — not as a value that gets listed on a company's about page, but as a structural requirement enforced by something other than the goodwill of the people in power. The book is an argument, made through lived experience, for why that enforcement has to be external. Internal accountability in a culture of carelessness is not accountability. It's theater. If you're building in tech — in security, in policy, in AI governance, in anything adjacent to how powerful systems get deployed at scale — this is not an optional read. Wynn-Williams earned this account. The least we can do is take it seriously. --- *Daniel Santiago is a cybersecurity practitioner focused on IAM, cloud security, and AI security governance.
